Websites and moneyWhat a website really costs on Maui

By Zachary Tye Wennstedt. Published September 20, 2026. About 11 minutes to read.

Build prices, the costs nobody mentions, and the simple math on when leasing a custom site beats writing one big check.

The short answer: a custom small-business website for a Hawaiʻi business typically costs between $2,500 and $12,000 to build, plus modest ongoing costs for your domain, hosting and updates. Or you can lease a custom site starting at $99 a month and buy it later. The right choice depends mostly on your cash flow, not the website.

Why website prices range so widely

Ask five people on Maui what a website costs and you'll hear five answers, from "my cousin did it for free" to "we paid twenty grand." They're all telling the truth. They're just describing different things.

A website isn't one product. It's a bundle of decisions, and each one moves the price. Here's what actually drives it:

  • Page count. A five-page service site is a very different job from a forty-page site with a page for every service and area you cover.
  • Writing. If you hand over finished text, it's cheaper. If someone has to interview you and write every page so it ranks and converts, that's real work, and it's usually the most valuable part.
  • Features. Online booking, rental calendars, menus, galleries, payment and quote forms all add time.
  • Custom code versus a template. Templates are cheaper up front. Custom code is faster, easier to rank, and yours to keep.
  • SEO built in or bolted on. A site built around the searches your customers type costs more than a pretty shell, and earns more.

On the low end, a focused single-location service site with clear, well-written pages sits near $2,500. On the high end, a multi-location or booking-heavy tourism site sits near $12,000. Beyond that, you're usually into complex custom software.

When leasing makes sense on Maui

Maui cash flow is seasonal. Winter and summer can be strong, and the shoulder months thin. That rhythm is exactly why a lease exists.

  • You're new. Keep your capital for inventory, equipment and payroll while the site starts bringing in work.
  • Your season is coming. Launch before whale season or the summer rush instead of waiting for a big month to pay for it.
  • You'd rather have predictable costs than a large one-time check.
  • You want the option to own it later. The buyout after 24 to 36 months is priced on the site's value, so a site that's earning its keep is worth owning.

A lease isn't a cheaper website. It's the same custom site, paid for the way your business actually earns.

When buying makes sense

  • You have the cash and want the asset on your books from day one.
  • You're planning to keep the site for many years with light changes.
  • You'd simply rather not have another monthly bill.

What about doing it yourself?

Do-it-yourself builders can work for a hobby or a brand-new side business. The monthly fee looks small. The real costs are the ones that don't show on the invoice:

  • Your time. Every hour you spend dragging boxes around is an hour not spent on customers.
  • Speed. Builder sites load extra code for features you'll never use, and slow sites lose visitors on island Wi-Fi.
  • Ranking. Getting the structure right for search takes know-how most builders won't teach you.
  • Ownership. Some platforms make it hard to leave with your site intact.

If you only need a basic page, a builder can be fine. If your business depends on being found by Maui customers, the website is where that happens, and it's worth doing right.

Seven questions to ask any web designer

  1. Will the domain and site be registered in my name?
  2. Is this custom code or a template, and can I move it later?
  3. Who writes the content, and is it written for search?
  4. How fast will it load on a phone, and how will you prove it?
  5. How are updates handled after launch, and what do they cost?
  6. Will it match my Google Business Profile exactly?
  7. Can you show me live sites that rank for real searches?

Three Maui scenarios

Numbers make more sense attached to real situations. These are illustrative examples of how different Maui businesses might approach a website, using the price ranges above. Your quote depends on your actual needs.

An Upcountry bed and breakfast

A small inn in Kula with four rooms needs a homepage, a page for the rooms, a page about the area, a booking link, a FAQ and a contact page. The owner has good photos but needs help with writing. This kind of site sits toward the lower-middle of the build range. Since bookings are seasonal and the owner would rather keep cash for a kitchen remodel, a lease makes sense, with a plan to buy the site outright once it's paying for itself.

A Kīhei food truck

A food truck needs a fast, simple site: a real-text menu, where the truck will be this week, hours, photos and a link to its Google profile. It's a small site near the bottom of the range, and the owner needs to update the location often. A small lease keeps costs low while the business is young, and quick updates on request handle the weekly location changes.

A Wailuku accounting firm

A three-partner firm wants a page for each service, bios for each partner, a secure-feeling contact process and content that ranks for tax and bookkeeping searches. There's more writing and more pages, putting it in the middle of the range. The firm has the budget and wants the asset on the books, so buying outright is the natural choice, paired with an SEO plan for tax season.

Same island, three very different answers. That's why "what does a website cost?" always starts with "what does your business need?"

What a good proposal includes

If you're comparing quotes, a clear proposal makes it easy to compare apples to apples. Look for:

  • A page list. Exactly which pages you're getting.
  • Who writes the content, and whether it's written for search.
  • Features spelled out: booking, forms, galleries, menus.
  • Timeline, with what's needed from you and when.
  • Ownership terms: who owns the domain, the site and the content.
  • After launch: hosting, updates, support and what each costs.
  • What's not included, so there are no surprises.

A proposal that's vague on any of these isn't necessarily a bad sign, but ask. A good designer will happily fill in the gaps.

Budgeting year by year

It helps to think of a website like a vehicle for your business: an upfront cost, then smaller ongoing costs to keep it running well. Over a typical first few years, expect your domain renewal every year, hosting every month or year, occasional content updates as your services and prices change, and a refresh of photos and key pages every couple of years. Businesses that set aside a small monthly amount for their site's upkeep rarely end up needing an expensive emergency rebuild.

A lease simplifies this, since hosting and care are typically bundled into the monthly price. When you buy outright, you'll handle those costs separately, which gives you more control and more to manage.

The bottom line

The cheapest website is the one that brings in customers. A site that costs twice as much but turns visitors into calls is the better deal every time. Price out the build, count the hidden costs, then pick the payment path that fits your cash flow.

And whatever you build, pair it with a plan to be found, because a great site nobody visits is just an expensive business card. That's what Maui SEO is for.

Website cost questions

How much does a small-business website cost in Hawaiʻi?

Custom builds typically run between $2,500 and $12,000, depending on page count, features and how much writing is needed. Ongoing domain, hosting and update costs are usually modest.

Is leasing a website a good idea?

It can be, especially for new or seasonal businesses. A lease spreads the cost into monthly payments starting at $99, gets a custom site working sooner, and includes an option to buy it after 24 to 36 months.

What's included in the monthly lease?

A custom-coded site built and kept running for your business. Monthly pricing depends on the site's size, complexity and level of optimization, and we quote it plainly before starting.

Should I own my domain name?

Always. Register the domain in your own name so you never lose your web address if you change designers.

What should a website proposal include?

A page list, who writes the content, the features included, a timeline, ownership terms for the domain and site, what happens after launch and what it costs, and anything that's not included.

How much does it cost to maintain a website each year?

Ongoing costs usually include a yearly domain renewal, hosting, and occasional updates. Lean custom sites keep these modest. With a lease, hosting and care are typically bundled into the monthly price.
